# Email

Two paths. Pick the one that matches what you were given.

- **Compose.** You were told what the email must achieve. Work through section 4, then write to the budgets in section 3.
- **Revise.** You were given a draft. Run section 14 in order and report what you changed and which rule drove it.

Both paths end at the same place: an email whose reader can act on it in under ten seconds.

The numbers below are not style preferences. They come from five studies of real mailboxes and are listed with their effect sizes in section 2, so that you can rank your advice instead of reciting all of it. When two rules conflict, the one with the larger effect wins.

## 1. When This Applies

Applies to email a person will read and may reply to: colleagues, an advisor, collaborators, reviewers, a hiring manager, someone you met at a conference, and first contact with a stranger (section 12).

Does not apply to automated mail, transactional receipts, or newsletters sent to a subscriber list. Those are read under different conditions and the numbers here do not transfer.

Ask before inventing. If you do not know who must act, what the deadline is, or whether the recipient is inside the same organization, ask the user. Do not fill those in with a plausible guess, because sections 4 and 7 depend on all three.

## 2. What the Numbers Say, Ranked

Effect sizes, largest first. "Reply rate" is the share of emails that received at least one reply, higher is better. "Reply probability" is one named recipient's chance of replying, from a regression that controls for the other columns.

| Factor | Measured effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recipient is inside your organization | 7.76% vs 2.26% reply rate | Yang 2017 |
| Body length 40 words vs 500 words | 9.64% vs 2.82% reply rate | Yang 2017 |
| Recipient's daily mail volume | reply share falls from 25% to under 5% at about 100 messages a day | Kooti 2015 |
| Email contains a request | 14.81% vs 7.45% reply rate | Yang 2017 |
| Sent in the afternoon vs at night | 7.77% vs 3.63% reply rate | Yang 2017 |
| Sent on a weekday vs a weekend | about 7% vs 4% reply rate | Yang 2017 |
| Contains a social line | +23% reply probability | Dabbish 2005 |
| Contains an information request (a question) | +22% reply probability | Dabbish 2005 |
| Sent to one person vs to a list | +20% vs -18% reply probability | Dabbish 2005 |
| Carries an attachment | +1.7 points of reply rate, and 23.81% longer to get a reply | Yang 2017 |
| Action request, status update, scheduling, reminder | no significant effect on reply probability | Dabbish 2005 |
| Font and font size | never measured; mostly replaced in transit (section 9) | Litmus |

Two consequences shape everything below.

The default outcome is silence. Overall reply rate in a 279-person corporate mailbox corpus was 6.53% to 7.70%, and recipients judged that 64% of the mail they received needed no reply at all. Moving a message from 6% to 9% is a real improvement and still leaves silence as the likely outcome. Do not promise the user that a rewrite will produce an answer.

Making a message feel important is not the same as getting it answered. The four content types with no measured effect on replies are exactly the ones that raise perceived importance the most: scheduling +24%, action request +20%, status update +18%, reminder +13%. A question is what gets answered.

## 3. Budget Card

Per email. The Chinese column is a content-density conversion, not a separate measurement.

| Element | Budget (English) | Budget (Chinese) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body | 40 to 50 words | 70 to 90 characters | Reply rate peaks near 40 words; the median reply people write is 43 words |
| Subject | 3 to 5 words | 10 to 15 characters | Reply rate falls as the subject lengthens |
| Names on the To line | 1 | 1 | One recipient +20%, a list -18% |
| Paragraphs | at most 3 | at most 3 | Over half of all reading events last under 10 seconds |
| Questions asked | exactly 1 | exactly 1 | More than one splits the answer and delays it |
| Greeting and sign-off | 1 line each | 1 line each | A social line raises reply probability 23% |
| Attachments | 0 when you want speed | 0 when you want speed | An attachment adds about 24% to reply time |
| Manual line breaks inside a paragraph | 0 | 0 | Section 8 |
| Font or size declarations | 0 | 0 | Section 9 |

Going over one budget is a trade, not an error. Going over three means the message should be a document, a meeting, or two separate emails.

## 4. Compose: Decide Four Things Before Writing

Settle these before the first sentence. Each one changes the draft.

1. **Who must act.** One name. If two people must each do something, that is two emails; a single message addressed to both loses 18% of its reply probability and lets each of them assume the other will answer.
2. **What the one question is.** Write it out as a sentence ending in a question mark, and confirm the recipient can answer it without opening anything. If you cannot state it, you are writing a status update, and status updates do not get replies. Say so to the user and ask what you actually want back.
3. **Inside or outside the organization.** Outside, expect roughly a third of the reply rate and follow section 12.
4. **Whether a date is real.** A real deadline goes in the body as a date. An invented one is pressure, and the recipient usually knows the difference.

Then write in this order: the fact or number, the choice it creates, the question. Greeting first, sign-off last, one line each.

## 5. The Body: One Fact, One Choice, One Question

The shape that fits the budget and matches how the evidence says people read:

```
<greeting, one line>

<the fact, with the number in it. Where the detail lives.>

<the choice, stated as two or three named options.>
<the question, answerable in one word or one sentence.>

<sign-off, one line>
```

Rules that follow from it:

- Put the number in the body. An attachment slows the reply by about 24%, so the attachment holds the detail and the body holds the figure the recipient needs in order to answer.
- Name the options. "Let me know what you think" is not a question. "Do you prefer A or B?" is.
- Put the ask in the last paragraph, never in the middle, and never only in the subject.
- Give the reader a way to decline. In a request to anyone who can refuse, phrase the ask as a question with room for no.

## 6. Content That Feels Important but Does Not Get Replies

Four content types raise how important the message looks and do not raise the chance of an answer: action requests, status updates, scheduling notices, and reminders.

This is the most common failure in a draft that already looks professional. The writer has written "Please review and advise," believes the message is now actionable, and has in fact asked nothing.

> **Don't:** Please review the attached results and advise on next steps.
> **Do:** The baseline came out 2.3 points below the published number. Do you want me to keep tuning, or switch datasets?

> **Don't:** Following up on my last message regarding the draft.
> **Do:** Is Thursday still workable for your comments on section 3, or should I plan around a later date?

The social line is the counterintuitive one. A greeting and a sign-off raise reply probability by 23%, even though recipients rate socially-toned messages as 32% less important. Do not delete them in the name of brevity. One line each, not a paragraph of pleasantries.

## 7. Recipients

Two findings that look contradictory and are not.

- One named recipient raises that person's chance of replying by 20%; sending to a list lowers it by 18% (Dabbish 2005).
- Mail sent to three to five addresses has the highest chance that *somebody* replies, about 10% (Yang 2017), while median reply time climbs from 44 minutes at one recipient to 95 minutes at eight.

They measure different outcomes. The first asks whether a given person replies, the second whether anyone does.

So: to get an answer from a specific person, put only that person on the To line and move everyone else to Cc. To get an answer from whoever is free, a small group works and will be slower. Never both, and never use Cc as pressure; more names on the message lowers each person's sense that the reply is theirs to write.

## 8. Formatting

**Never insert manual line breaks inside a paragraph.** Write each paragraph as one long line and let the recipient's client wrap it.

A manual break is a real character in the message and the receiving client cannot remove it. Breaks placed to make lines about 70 characters wide get wrapped a second time on a phone that fits 40, producing alternating long and short lines. Plain-text mail has a fix for this, the `format=flowed` marker defined in [RFC 3676](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc3676), where a trailing space marks a line the client may re-wrap. It does not help in practice, because most mail today is HTML, where a manual break becomes a `<br>` element that is explicitly not re-wrappable.

Skimmability comes from structure, not from hand-placed breaks:

- One idea per paragraph, two or three sentences, then a blank line. A blank line is a paragraph boundary and survives every screen width.
- Parallel items go in a real numbered or bulleted list, so the client renders it.
- The thing you want done goes in its own paragraph, first or last.
- Three paragraphs maximum. Past that, the subject is not an email.

Two exceptions where manual wrapping is correct, both involving fixed-width readers rather than people: plain-text technical mailing lists, which usually ask for 72 to 80 columns, and patches sent with `git send-email`.

## 9. Fonts and Size

Do not set a font, a size, or a color. Send with the client's default body style.

No study has connected typeface to reply rate, and the setting largely does not survive the trip. Web fonts are supported in Apple Mail, Outlook for Mac and iOS, and not supported in Gmail on web or mobile, Outlook 2013 through 2021 on Windows, Office 365, Outlook.com, Yahoo Mail, or Samsung Mail ([Litmus](https://www.litmus.com/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-web-fonts)). When unsupported, each client falls back to its own default: Gmail to Arial, Apple Mail to Helvetica, Outlook to Times New Roman. Outlook additionally ignores your fallback list entirely if the font arrived by `<link>` or `@import`.

Monospace carries a second cost beyond appearance: it fits fewer characters per line, so it triggers re-wrapping on narrow screens sooner, and it is not universally supported either.

If the user wants larger text for their own reading, that is a local display setting and has nothing to do with replies. On macOS it is Mail, Settings, Fonts and Colors ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/mail/change-fonts-colors-settings-cpmlpreffont/mac)). Say plainly that it changes only what they see.

## 10. Subject Line

Three to five words naming the thing the message is about. Reply rate falls from 11.47% at a one-word subject to 1.10% at 29 words (Yang 2017).

Read that finding carefully before repeating it. The authors note that many very long subjects are machine-generated notices with empty bodies, which nobody would answer regardless, so most of that tenfold gap is not caused by length. The safe instruction is "short and specific." The unsafe one is "shortening the subject multiplies your reply rate."

**Never fabricate `Re:` on a first message.** It does two things, and neither is the intended one.

It does not thread anything. Mail clients group a conversation using the `In-Reply-To` and `References` header fields, which carry the `Message-ID` of the parent message ([RFC 5322](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5322), section 3.6.4). Typing three characters into the subject generates neither header, so no client will group the message.

For commercial email it is illegal. The US Federal Trade Commission's CAN-SPAM guidance requires that header information be accurate and that "The subject line must accurately reflect the content of the message," with penalties up to USD 53,088 per message ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). For non-commercial mail the cost is trust instead: once a recipient finds one fake `Re:`, every later subject line from that sender is discounted.

A genuine `Re:` added by the client when replying is correct and should be left alone.

## 11. Timing

Send on a weekday, during the recipient's working hours, and prefer the afternoon.

| Sent | Reply rate | Median time to reply |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00 to 06:00 | 3.63% | over 7 hours |
| 06:00 to 12:00 | about 7% | under 1 hour |
| 12:00 to 18:00 | 7.77% | under 1 hour |
| 18:00 to 24:00 | about 5% | over 7 hours |

Weekdays run about 7% against 4% at weekends, and median reply time for a Saturday send is roughly 30 times longer than for a weekday send, 13 times for a Sunday.

Two cautions when advising on this. The published data is bucketed into six-hour blocks, so there is no evidence for any claim about a specific hour; do not tell the user that 11:00 beats 14:00. And this factor is smaller than body length or whether the recipient is inside the organization, so do not let a timing discussion displace those.

The practical instruction is to write whenever, and schedule the send. Every major client supports it. Never advise waiting at a keyboard for a window.

## 12. Cold Outreach to a Stranger

Same rules, one third the expected return: 2.26% reply rate to an external address against 7.76% internal. Set that expectation with the user before drafting.

What is worth spending the 40-word budget on, in order:

1. One sentence saying why you are writing to this person specifically, naming the paper, the repository, the talk, or the shared contact. A reason that would fit any recipient is the same as no reason.
2. One question they can answer from what they already know, with no homework attached.
3. An explicit exit, such as saying that no reply is a fine answer. It costs four words and removes the obligation that makes people postpone.

Do not use a vague subject line to manufacture curiosity. One laboratory study found that messages with no subject were read 56% of the time against 38% for messages with one ([Wainer 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1978942.1979456)), and it is a weak basis for action: it measured opening rather than replying, the effect nearly disappeared in the high-load condition that resembles a real inbox, and the senders were fictional, so no reputation was at stake. Repeated contact with the same person makes it a losing trade.

**If the message is commercial, CAN-SPAM applies and the requirements are not optional.** The FTC lists them as: accurate header information; a subject line that reflects the content; clear disclosure that the message is an advertisement; a valid physical postal address; a visible way to opt out; honoring opt-outs within 10 business days and keeping the mechanism live at least 30 days; and responsibility for anything a contractor sends on your behalf. Penalties reach USD 53,088 per message. If the user is sending anything promotional to people who did not ask for it, say this before drafting, not after.

## 13. Worked Example

Before. Four recipients, 173 Chinese characters of body, a fabricated `Re:`, the number hidden in an attachment, and no question.

## 14. Revision Pass

Work in this order, largest effect first. Each step is mechanical. Report each change and the rule behind it.

1. Count the names on the To line. More than one, and the reply probability drops 18%. Move everyone who does not have to act to Cc. If two people must each act, split the message.
2. Find the question mark. Exactly one, in the last paragraph, answerable in one sentence without opening the attachment. None means the draft is a status update and will not get a reply; say so.
3. Count the body words, excluding greeting and sign-off. Budget is 40 to 50 English words, or 70 to 90 Chinese characters. Cut to the number before anything else; the first pass usually removes a third.
4. Read the subject alone. Three to five words, truthful, no fabricated `Re:`, and it names the thing rather than promising news.
5. Confirm the greeting and sign-off are present, one line each. Restore them if a previous edit removed them for brevity.
6. Move every number the recipient needs in order to answer out of the attachment and into the body. If the attachment then holds nothing they need, drop it and save about 24% of the reply time.
7. Find manual line breaks inside paragraphs and delete them, joining each paragraph into one line. Keep blank lines between paragraphs.
8. Count paragraphs. Three or fewer. Convert any hand-made list into a real numbered or bulleted list.
9. Strip font, size, and color declarations. Send with the client's default.
10. Check the send time. Weekday, recipient's working hours, afternoon preferred. Outside that, schedule it instead of sending.
11. If the recipient is a stranger, apply section 12, and if the message is commercial, walk the CAN-SPAM list before sending.
12. Read the draft once as the recipient, with their workload in mind. If the ask reads as an obligation rather than a question, rewrite the last paragraph.

A mechanical check on a draft file, if useful:

```bash
f=draft.txt

wc -w < "$f"                    # body budget: 40-50 English words
python3 -c "import re,sys;print(len(re.sub(r'\s','',open(sys.argv[1]).read())))" "$f"   # Chinese: 70-90

rg -o '\?' "$f" | wc -l         # expect exactly 1
rg -c '^\s*$' "$f"              # blank lines: paragraphs = this + 1, want <= 3

# candidate manual line breaks: a line followed by more text but not ending a sentence
awk 'p!="" && $0!="" && p !~ /[.!?:;]$/ {print NR-1": "p} {p=$0}' "$f"

rg -i 'font-family|font-size|<font|style="[^"]*font' "$f"   # expect no matches
rg -i '^subject:.*\bre:' "$f"   # a fabricated Re: on a first message
```

## 15. Do Not Overcorrect

- Do not promise a reply. The measured baseline is 6% to 8%, and recipients judge 64% of their mail as needing no answer. A good rewrite improves the odds and does not create an obligation.
- Do not read the correlations as causes. Four of the five studies are observational. "Messages near 40 words have the highest reply rate" is supported; "shortening this message to 40 words will raise its reply rate" is an extrapolation. Long messages and low reply rates share causes, such as being complicated or being an announcement.
- Do not cut below the budget. A one-word body had a 4.65% reply rate, lower than a 200-word one. Too short fails for the opposite reason: the reader cannot tell what is being asked.
- Do not delete the greeting. It is the one piece of apparent padding the evidence defends.
- Do not spend the conversation on timing or fonts. They are the two smallest factors on the list, and fonts are not a factor at all.
- Do not over-apply the samples. The corporate corpus is English mail from a US technology company in 2000 and 2001, before smartphones; one dataset is personal rather than work mail; one is a 124-person self-report survey, where what people say they do differs from what they do; one is a 38-person laboratory simulation. None of them measured present-day Chinese workplace email.
- Do not ignore what the user cannot change. Whether the recipient is inside the same organization (a factor of 3.4) and how much mail they receive (25% down to under 5%) outweigh every writing rule here. When a draft is already clean, say that the remaining constraint is the relationship, not the prose.
